Maximum cable height with wind

Find the greatest height allowed by the cable length and the upper tension limit. The lift-off angle is calculated; excess cable can rest on the ground. Wind blows from the winch toward the aircraft (+x).

Use either a dot or a comma for decimals (for example, 0.1 or 0,1). Do not use thousands separators.

Enter the speed shown on the glider's airspeed indicator. The calculator estimates true airspeed (TAS) from standard-atmosphere density at the glider's calculated altitude. The glider flies through the air toward the winch and upward at the entered angle. Wind can carry it away from the winch over the ground even while it climbs. Set IAS to 0 for the stationary-cable model. FAA airspeed explanation.

This is a quasi-static height estimate, not a time-dependent launch simulation. A moving cable's velocity is approximated as rising linearly from zero at the winch to the glider's ground velocity. The entered climb angle sets the glider's direction through the air, not its ground track or a fixed endpoint position. Cable acceleration, winch payout, and the glider's own lift and drag are not modeled.

How it is calculated

The winch is at (0, 0). The upper tension is a limit, not a force that must be used. The calculation searches for the highest quasi-static cable path with a nondecreasing horizontal position, while keeping the upper tension at or below that limit. When the whole cable cannot be lifted, the unused length lies level on the ground and the suspended cable leaves the ground horizontally. Ground friction and winch torque are not included.

w = μg,   g = 9.81 m/s²;   L = Lground + Lsuspended.
Local altitude = start altitude + y(s). Temperature T(s), pressure and air density ρ(s) follow the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere at that altitude.
Wind profile: H = min(1,000 m, max(10 m, ytop));   α = ln(2) / ln(100) ≈ 0.1505;
U(y) = Utop [min(max(y, 10 m), H) / H]α. The calculated top height and wind profile are solved together.
Re⊥ = ρ(s) |A⊥| d / μair(s);   CD = f(Re⊥) from a circular-cylinder correlation.
At glider altitude, TAS ≈ IAS × √(ρsea level / ρtop). This low-speed estimate treats IAS as calibrated/equivalent airspeed; instrument, position and compressibility corrections are omitted.
For glider true airspeed Va = TAS and air-relative climb angle γ, glider ground velocity is vg = (Utop − Va cos γ, Va sin γ). Positive horizontal velocity means drifting away from the winch.
Estimated local cable velocity = (s / Lsuspended)vg; local relative airflow A = (U(y), 0) − cable velocity.
True crossflow A⊥ = A − (A · t)t; sea-level-equivalent crossflow = |A⊥|√(ρ(s)/ρsea level). The cable load uses the true crossflow: q = ½ρ(s)CDd |A⊥| A⊥,   qx = x-component of q.
Suspended-cable equilibrium: r′ = P/|P|,   P′ = (0, w) − q;   maximize ytop subject to |Ptop| ≤ Tlimit;   total Fx = ∫ qx ds.
Ideal no-wind ceiling: hmax = min(L, Tlimit / (μg)).

The wind profile is an illustrative power-law approximation: for a top at or above 1,000 m, the 10 m speed is half the entered top speed, including for a top at 5,500 m. Wind is held at its 10 m value below 10 m and at its top value above H. The 1,000 m layer depth and 2:1 ratio are modeling assumptions; actual wind profiles depend on terrain, weather and atmospheric stability. The height y is above the start point. Temperature, pressure and air density follow the dry-air U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976 (15 °C and 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level), using its 0–11 km, 11–20 km, and 20–32 km geopotential-altitude layers. This is a reference profile, not a weather forecast. Air viscosity uses the local temperature and Sutherland's law; the drag coefficient uses the piecewise infinite-cylinder fit in NASA/CR–2012–217665, Table 3.5, with Reynolds number limited to its stated range. This assumes an approximately smooth, circular, unstretched cable. The moving-cable velocity ramp is an engineering approximation; actual braid roughness, gusts and cable motion can change loads. Axial surface friction and ground friction are omitted.
